Detection modelling

What a sequence model adds to the Wazuh lab, measured on 2.6 million real alerts, and what it does not add

Step 5 of the Wazuh and MITRE ATT&CK lab · %(generated)s · Prepared by Koustub

Step 4 of this lab detects single events. Rule 100110 counts one failed SSH password; rule 100112 notices an account being created. Neither knows the other exists, and neither knows what an ordinary Tuesday looks like on the endpoint. This step is the attempt to add the thing they cannot do, which is to read a run of alerts and judge it.

The result is that a sequence model does not earn its place here. Measured on %(alerts_total)s real alerts from eight independent networks, plain logistic regression on rule counts and timing beats a GRU over the alert sequence on %(wins_log)s held out networks out of %(n_folds)s. The GRU wins none of them, trailing by a mean of %(portable_gap_gru)s average precision. On this evidence PyTorch is not justified for this problem.

The model that is deployed on the lab dashboard is therefore a logistic regression over eleven features, and it is deployed with its measurement attached rather than as a detection.

1. Why a model was tried at all

The rules in step 4 are good at what they do and structurally blind to sequence. A brute force attempt followed four minutes later by a new account and a cron entry is three separate alerts to a Wazuh manager, and an analyst reading a queue has to assemble that story by hand. The question worth asking is whether something that reads the window rather than the event can order that queue better than the rules already do.

That question has a clear failure condition, and stating it first is the point. A model that cannot beat the single best rule is not adding anything, and it is easy to build one that looks impressive and is not. The comparison below is arranged so that a bad result is visible rather than avoidable.

2. The dataset

Collecting a labelled dataset from this lab means a 14 hour campaign, and public data was checked first. Kaggle and Hugging Face were both searched and neither has anything usable for this problem: what is there is network flow tables, which are the wrong telemetry, or small instruction tuning sets, or synthetic data under a licence that forbids redistribution. There is one public dataset of real Wazuh alerts with ground truth labels.

The AIT Alert Data Set (Zenodo record 8263181, CC-BY-4.0, published with the CSET 2024 paper) is alerts from eight simulated enterprise networks, each carrying a labelled multi-step intrusion. The alerts are in native alerts.json form, which is the same record this lab's own manager writes. The import read %(alerts_total)s alert records out of the archive.

What this dataset cannot answer, stated plainly. It is not a test of this lab's rules. None of rules 100100 to 100113 have a parent signature anywhere in it: no sshd brute force in any of the eight scenarios, no local account creation, no file integrity monitoring. Rules 5501 and 5502 are the entire overlap, and what AIT calls password cracking is offline hash cracking and WPScan rather than SSH. So everything below measures the method. Only a campaign on this lab measures the lab, and that is section 9.

How an episode is built

An episode is a fixed %(window)s second window of alerts, labelled attack if it overlaps a ground truth attack phase from the dataset's own labels.csv. Tumbling rather than sliding, so no alert is counted twice and no two episodes share evidence. The tail of an over-long window is kept rather than the head, because persistence lands at the end of a window. The choice of width was checked rather than assumed: logistic average precision is 0.296, 0.303 and 0.289 at 120, 300 and 600 second windows, so the conclusions here are not an artefact of where the boundaries fall.

3. The training process

Four decisions do more work than the model choice, and all four are arranged to make a flattering result harder to obtain.

Whole networks are held out, never a random sample

Episodes from one capture are a time series. Shuffling before splitting lets the model see the future: the test set would sit between training examples drawn minutes either side of it, and the score would improve for a reason that has nothing to do with detection. Each AIT scenario is a separate network, so whole networks are held out instead. A model that scores well on a network it has never seen has generalised. A model that scores well on the last hour of a network it trained on may only have learned that network's noise floor.

Eight folds, not one split

On a single split the logistic model and the GRU finished 0.046 apart with forty attack episodes between them, which is well inside what a different pair of held out networks would move. Every result below is leave one network out across all %(n_folds)s networks: train on seven, test on the eighth, repeat. Counting how many folds each model wins is the claim that survives.

Average precision, not f1 at a fixed cutoff

At a %(base_pct)s %% base rate, f1 at 0.5 says more about where a model happens to put its threshold than about whether it ranks attacks above background. The same logistic predictions score f1 0.049 at 0.5 and 0.367 at a threshold chosen on validation. Average precision is the area under the precision-recall curve and cannot be flattered that way. Its floor is the base rate: a model ranking at random scores %(base_ap)s here.

Nothing is chosen on the test set

The vocabulary of known signatures is built from training sources only, so a signature that appears solely in the held out network falls through to an unknown index, which is the correct thing for it to do. Thresholds and early stopping are chosen on a validation slice held back from training. The GRU is run with three seeds per fold and the mean reported, because a single seed on %(attack)s positives is noise.

5. Which model won, and what that means

Logistic regression on rule counts and timing wins all %(wins_log)s folds. The GRU beats it on none of them. That is the finding, and three things follow from it that are not all negative.

All of them find real signal. %(log_ap)s against a %(base_ap)s base rate is roughly twelve times better than chance, and the single best rule at %(rule_ap)s is not far behind. The signal is there. The argument is only about what is needed to extract it.

The sequence model is not obviously wrong, it is just not worth it. The GRU reaches %(gru_ap)s, above the best single rule, so ordering by sequence does carry information. It costs 5,458 parameters, a PyTorch dependency and a training loop to land below twenty numbers fitted by gradient descent in a fifth of a second. A fair reading is that a small recurrent model does not beat counting on this data, not that no sequence model ever could: this one had no class-balanced sampling, no per-fold threshold tuning during training and no attention over the window.

None of them is deployable as an alerting rule. Take the winning model on its best fold, %(op_held)s, which is %(op_n)s windows containing %(op_attack)s attack windows. Ranked by score, it holds perfect precision down to recall %(op_perfect_rec)s: %(op_perfect_tp)s attacks caught and nothing false. Push it to catch half of them, recall %(op_half_rec)s, and precision is %(op_half_prec)s, which is %(op_half_tp)s real attacks arriving with %(op_half_fp)s false positives beside them. That is what triage looks like at a two percent base rate, and it is the honest correction to section 7.

6. What was deployed, and why it is not the winner

The model that won every fold cannot be run on this lab, and that is worth being precise about. Its feature vector is one column per AIT rule id. This lab and AIT share two signatures out of %(signatures)s. Pointed at live lab alerts, every one of them would land in the unknown column and the model would return a confident number about nothing.

So a second feature set was fitted: eleven columns that describe a window without naming a single signature. How many alerts arrived, over how long, how close together, how tightly the busiest minute was packed, how many distinct signatures were involved, and how severe they were. Severity earns its place by being a property of the Wazuh ruleset rather than of one capture, which is why it transfers where a rule id does not; it is worth roughly double on its own.

The gap of %(portable_gap)s average precision against the full feature set is the measured price of portability, and it is not small. Most of the signal in the full model was in which rules fired, not in the shape of the burst, which is itself an argument for the rule based approach this lab already has.

This is the model on the dashboard. It is fitted on %(m_train_eps)s episodes from %(m_train_nets)s networks, with its cutoff of %(m_thr)s chosen on %(m_thr_eps)s episodes from %(m_thr_nets)s, which the fit never saw, where it reached precision %(m_thr_prec)s at recall %(m_thr_rec)s. It runs inside the SSH poll the dashboard was already making, as a dot product in plain Python, so nothing is installed on the manager and no PyTorch goes near a box whose job is receiving alerts.

What the dashboard panel claims, and what it does not. The panel prints the model's provenance and its measured average precision underneath itself, permanently, along with the sentence that it has never been measured on this lab. It presents the score as triage ordering and not as a detection. Bars are scaled against the model's cutoff rather than against the tallest bar on screen, because auto-scaling a narrow band of scores turns a 0.03 spread into a dramatic climb, which is a picture of noise.

What the dashboard actually shows, which is not the model's number

One more step sits between the model and the panel, and it was added because the model's own output is the wrong thing to put in front of an analyst. It answers how unusual a window is. It does not answer how bad it is, and forty level 3 alerts look much like one level 12 beside a new account to something reading only shape and severity mass.

So the panel leads with a 0 to 100 severity, a composite of six terms with the model as one of them, weighted %(w_model)s. The others are the highest rule level in the window, how much high level alerting there was rather than just the peak, how tightly the busiest minute was packed, how many distinct signatures were involved, and how many attack stages appeared. The total is multiplied by up to %(chain_max)s when credential access and persistence both appear, because that pairing is a chain rather than two events, and a chain is exactly what six single event rules cannot see between them.

On a fabricated run used to test the path end to end, a quiet window of session opens scores %(sev_quiet)s, a brute force burst on its own scores %(sev_brute)s, and the same burst with an account creation beside it scores %(sev_chain)s. Step 4 cannot separate the last two: it reports a brute force alert and an account creation alert, and nothing that says they belong together.

Every constant in that composite is judgement rather than a fitted parameter, and the panel says so. They are in one file, scoring/scorer/score.py, and the panel prints the weights it was actually given rather than a copy, so the explanation on screen cannot drift from the arithmetic behind it. A window reaching 50 becomes a finding, and findings export to their own PDF.

The generator builds in exactly two signals, that an attacker brute forces before persisting and moves between steps in seconds, and both are linearly separable from counts and a burst measure. A good score there was evidence that the pipeline ran, not that anything had been detected. Real alerts cost the GRU %(syn_drop)s of its f1.

Reporting this is the point of including it. A model evaluated only on data produced by assumptions it then recovers will always look excellent, and the difference between %(syn_gru_ap)s and %(gru_ap)s is the entire distance between a demo and a measurement.

8. What this does not support

  • It is not a test of this lab's detections. Rules 100100 to 100113 appear in no public dataset. Nothing here says whether they separate an attacker from an administrator in sequence.
  • It is not an alerting capability. At this base rate the best operating point found catches a fifth of intrusions at perfect precision, or drowns the queue. The deployed model is ordering, not detection.
  • The deployed model has never been measured on this network. Its %(shape_ap)s is what it scored on networks it had not seen. This lab is a ninth such network, so that is the honest expectation for it and not a result from it.
  • The GRU has not been given every chance. The conclusion is that a small recurrent model does not beat counting here, on this data, at this scale.
  • Windows scenarios are untouched. Rules 100100 to 100103 are unreachable from a Linux campaign.

9. What would change the answer

A campaign on the lab itself. run-campaign.sh drives the three scenarios against both endpoints alongside three standing accounts with deliberately different working shapes, and produces alerts carrying this lab's own rule ids with ground truth labels attached. That converts three of the five limitations above into measurements: whether the lab's rules separate in sequence, whether a per-user pattern of life helps, and what the deployed model actually scores here rather than elsewhere.

It was trialled successfully for one hour on 17 September. The full run is 14 hours. It is now a specific question rather than a blocker, which is the main thing the public data bought.
